## What is GRC?

GRC (Governance, Risk and Compliance) is an integrated discipline covering how organisations are directed and controlled, how they identify and manage risk, and how they ensure conformance with applicable laws, standards, and obligations. The three pillars are distinct but mutually reinforcing.

- **Governance** — structures, accountabilities, policies, and oversight processes that direct strategic and operational decisions.
- **Risk management** — systematic identification, assessment, treatment, and monitoring of threats to objectives and opportunities for improvement.
- **Compliance** — ongoing conformance with laws, regulations, industry standards, contracts, and internal policies.

Organisations pursue integrated GRC to break down siloes, reduce duplicated assurance effort, improve risk-based decision-making, and provide coherent reporting to boards and regulators.

## GRC Frameworks and Standards

### COSO ERM

The Committee of Sponsoring Organisations Enterprise Risk Management framework is widely adopted in listed companies and financial services. The 2017 update integrates risk management with strategy-setting. Core components: Governance and Culture; Strategy and Objective-Setting; Performance; Review and Revision; Information, Communication and Reporting.

### ISO 31000 — Risk Management

ISO 31000:2018 provides principles, a framework, and a process for risk management applicable across any organisation or sector. It is a guidance standard, not certifiable, but widely used as a reference and baseline.

### ISO 27001 — Information Security Management Systems

ISO 27001 is a certifiable standard for information security management. Risk assessment and treatment are core requirements. Annex A contains 93 controls across four themes: Organisational, People, Physical, and Technological. Widely held in financial services, professional services, and technology.

### NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology CSF organises cybersecurity activities around five functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Widely adopted globally. CSF 2.0 (2024) adds a sixth function — Govern — making it more aligned with enterprise GRC approaches.

### COBIT

Control Objectives for Information and Related Technology. An IT governance and management framework covering 40 management objectives and 5 governance objectives. Used by organisations with significant technology risk or where IT governance accountability needs to be formalised.

### Three Lines of Defence (or Three Lines Model)

A foundational governance model for risk accountability:

- **First Line** — Business units and functions that own and manage risk in day-to-day operations. Responsible for implementing controls.
- **Second Line** — Risk, compliance, and control functions that provide oversight, guidance, and challenge to the first line. Risk managers, compliance officers, information security teams.
- **Third Line** — Internal audit, which provides independent assurance to the board and senior management on the effectiveness of governance, risk management, and control.

External audit, regulators, and other external assurance providers sit outside the three lines.

## Risk Management Lifecycle

1. **Context establishment** — Understanding the organisation, its objectives, stakeholders, risk appetite, and external environment.
2. **Risk identification** — Surfacing potential events or conditions that could affect objectives. Methods: workshops, interviews, process walkthroughs, scenario analysis, KRI trend review, horizon scanning.
3. **Risk assessment** — Evaluating likelihood and impact to determine risk level (inherent risk = before controls; residual risk = after controls). Scales: 3×3, 4×4, 5×5 heat maps. Qualitative or quantitative.
4. **Risk treatment** — Selecting a response:
   - **Accept** — Risk is within appetite; no action required.
   - **Avoid** — Cease the activity that creates the risk.
   - **Reduce/Mitigate** — Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact.
   - **Transfer** — Shift the risk via insurance, contracts, or outsourcing.
5. **Control implementation** — Preventive (stop the event), Detective (identify when it has occurred), Corrective (repair the damage).
6. **Monitoring and reporting** — KRIs and KCIs tracked over time. Regular reporting to risk committees, senior management, and boards.
7. **Review and improvement** — Periodic reassessment following audits, incidents, environmental change, or the programme review cycle.

## Risk Appetite and Tolerance

**Risk appetite** is the amount and type of risk an organisation is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives. It should be approved by the board and linked to strategy.

**Risk tolerance** is the acceptable variation around a specific risk or objective — the operational boundary within which risk appetite is applied.

Organisations express risk appetite through qualitative statements, quantitative thresholds, or both. Well-defined appetite enables the first line to make risk decisions without escalation for every issue.

## GRC Programme Architecture

### Risk Universe and Taxonomy

A risk taxonomy classifies risks into categories and sub-categories shared across the organisation. Common top-level categories: Strategic, Operational, Financial, Compliance, Technology/Cyber, Reputational, Legal. A shared taxonomy enables consistent assessment and portfolio aggregation.

### Risk and Control Self-Assessment (RCSA)

Business units identify their risks and assess control effectiveness. Results feed the risk register. RCSAs are typically annual or triggered by significant change. The RCSA process is most valuable when owned by the business, not just completed as a compliance exercise.

### Key Risk Indicators (KRIs) and Key Control Indicators (KCIs)

- **KRIs** — metrics that signal changing risk exposure before losses occur. Effective KRIs are quantifiable, timely, and causally linked to a risk.
- **KCIs** — metrics that measure whether controls are operating effectively.

Both are reported to risk committees and boards on a scheduled basis.

### Policy Framework

Most organisations maintain a layered policy structure:
- **Board Policies** — High-level mandatory statements setting direction and tone.
- **Standards** — Specific requirements that implement board policies.
- **Procedures and Guidelines** — Operational how-to documentation for staff.

## GRC Technology and Platforms

GRC platforms consolidate risk registers, control libraries, compliance obligations, audit management, incident tracking, and reporting in a single connected system.

**Benefits:**
- Eliminates spreadsheet-based risk registers and email-based workflows.
- Automated workflow for risk reviews, control testing, and policy attestation.
- Real-time dashboards and board-level reporting.
- Cross-domain linkage: one control mapped to multiple risks and obligations.
- Complete audit trail and evidence management.

**Common platforms:** LogicGate Risk Cloud, ServiceNow GRC, Riskonnect, OneTrust, MetricStream, Archer (legacy).

## GRC Maturity Model

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 — Ad hoc | Reactive, undocumented, spreadsheet-dependent |
| 2 — Defined | Documented processes, some standardisation |
| 3 — Managed | Measured, consistent, some integration |
| 4 — Optimised | Continuous improvement, integrated, data-driven |

Most mid-market organisations beginning a GRC programme are at Level 1–2. Typical implementation targets reach Level 3 within 12–18 months.

## Common GRC Programme Challenges

- Fragmented data across spreadsheets, email chains, and legacy systems.
- Risk registers not updated between annual cycles.
- Low first-line engagement — business owners see GRC as a compliance burden.
- Compliance obligations mapped inconsistently or incompletely to controls.
- Audit findings not linked back to the risk register.
- Board reporting that does not reflect operational reality.
- Lack of automation leading to manual, error-prone processes.
- GRC programme managed in silos — risk, compliance, and audit not integrated.
